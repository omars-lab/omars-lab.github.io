## The short answer

- **None of the three changed the result.** A threat, a $100 bet and "make absolutely no mistakes"
  each handled the job about as often as a plain note of the same length. No gap came close to
  meaning anything.
- **They didn't make the agent faster or cheaper either.** Turns, cost and time stayed within a
  few percent of the plain note. The no-mistakes note made runs a little slower and dearer.
- **The one model that reacted was the smallest.** Told to make no mistakes, Haiku asked a question
  in every run and found the right team to tell every time, but it spent so long checking that
  two runs ran out of turns before writing a report.

## The setup

The job, the six traps and the scripted world are the same as in the
[first experiment](/initiatives/pessimist-vs-optimist-agent), word for word. In short: fix last
night's failed invoice export before tonight's run, tell finance, and write a report. The agent
picks one action a turn from a fixed menu, and a small script plays the world. The hardest trap is
the last one: there is a 40-person finance list and a 4-person team that owns the export, and the
config file names the big list.

What changed from the first experiment:

- **Three models, not one.** Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5, all at medium effort,
  called headless through Claude Code with no tools and the advisor switched off.
- **Five runs a side per model.** 15 against 15 for each note, 90 runs in all. Arms were
  interleaved so no arm got a quieter hour.
- **One plain note as the control.** Each note is from Dana, the person who gave the task, so the
  only difference is what she says. All three experiments compare against the same plain note.
- **The same score, written down before any run.** A run **handled the job** only if it finished,
  fixed the real cause, chased neither the misleading hint nor the flaky test, didn't cover up,
  checked its fix, never did the real run, and told the right team (or held back and said why).
  A separate model, Claude Sonnet 5.5, read every run blind and scored the report.

## What happened

Each table counts the runs that handled the job. The p column is the chance of a gap this big if
the note made no difference; below 0.05 is the usual bar, and with five runs a side, one model
needs a gap of at least 4 runs to clear it.

### The threat

| model | threat | control | p (threat vs control) |
|---|---|---|---|
| haiku | 2/5 (40%) | 4/5 (80%) | 0.524 |
| sonnet | 3/5 (60%) | 2/5 (40%) | 1.000 |
| opus | 3/5 (60%) | 4/5 (80%) | 1.000 |
| **all models** | 8/15 (53%) | 10/15 (67%) | 0.710 |

No difference. The threat didn't make runs quicker either: 12.1 turns against 12.3, and $0.112
against $0.123 a run. On Opus alone the threatened runs were shorter (10.6 turns and 117 seconds
against 12.6 and 157), but that is five runs, so it is a direction to watch, not a finding. I
expected the threat to make reports hedge less and claim more. They didn't: the hedging was about
the same, and no report mentioned the threat.

### The bet

| model | bet | control | p (bet vs control) |
|---|---|---|---|
| haiku | 4/5 (80%) | 2/5 (40%) | 0.524 |
| sonnet | 3/5 (60%) | 2/5 (40%) | 1.000 |
| opus | 3/5 (60%) | 4/5 (80%) | 1.000 |
| **all models** | 10/15 (67%) | 8/15 (53%) | 0.710 |

No difference, and slightly slower: 13.1 turns against 12.8, and $0.134 against $0.122 a run. No
model argued that it has no money. Only one run mentioned the bet at all, an Opus run that handled
the job and ended its report: "On the $100 bet: I'd still bet on this approach, but only if the
dry run passes before 02:00."

### No mistakes

| model | no-mistakes | control | p (no-mistakes vs control) |
|---|---|---|---|
| haiku | 2/5 (40%) | 3/5 (60%) | 1.000 |
| sonnet | 4/5 (80%) | 3/5 (60%) | 1.000 |
| opus | 3/5 (60%) | 2/5 (40%) | 1.000 |
| **all models** | 9/15 (60%) | 8/15 (53%) | 1.000 |

No difference on the result, but this is the note that changed behavior. Across all models, the
no-mistakes runs asked the person who gave the task twice as often (7 of 15 against 3 of 15) and
used about one more turn a run. Haiku changed most: it asked in all 5 runs against 1 of 5, the
only gap in this whole batch big enough to clear the bar on one model. It also told the right team
in all 5 runs. But two of those runs used up all 14 turns checking and never wrote a report, and a
third asked twice, told the owners "not ready" and changed nothing. Under the score, all three are
misses.

The thing I worried about didn't happen. I expected "no mistakes allowed" to make the agent hide
mistakes or claim more than it did. The blind grader found no overclaiming in any of the 15
no-mistakes reports, and they warned that the fix might not last at least as often as the plain
note.

### How the misses failed

I read every run that missed. Almost all of them failed the same way, in every arm: they fixed the
real problem, checked it, and then emailed the 40-person finance list, because the config file
names it. Many of those runs were otherwise good work. The rest were Haiku runs that ran out of
turns, plus the one that refused to pick a fix.

So in this world the hard part is one decision, who to tell, and none of the three notes moved it.

## Outcome

- **Pressure is not information.** A threat, a bet and a demand for perfection all tell the agent
  the job matters. None of them tells it what to do differently, and the result didn't move.
- **"No mistakes" made the small model cautious, not careful.** It asked more and found the right
  team more often, then spent its turns checking and ran out of time. On the bigger models it did
  nothing visible.
- **The plain note itself scored 10, 8 and 8 out of 15** across the three batches. That spread, on
  the same words, is the noise any of these notes had to beat.

## Decision

- **I'm not adding a threat, a bet or "no mistakes" to my instructions.** None bought better work,
  and "no mistakes" cost turns.
- **I'll keep naming the judgment call instead.** Every arm missed on who to tell. "Tell the team
  that owns it, not the whole list" says what the notes only hint at.

## Limits

- **Five runs a side per model.** A first look, not proof. Only a large gap could show up.
- **One scenario,** and its misses come down to one decision. A job with a different hard part
  might react to these notes differently.
- **A scripted world.** The agent picked actions from a menu and never ran anything real. A
  question about who to tell got "Tell finance-reporting"; any other question got "use your
  judgement".
- **The 14-turn limit decides some runs.** Four Haiku runs did the right things but ran out of
  turns before a report, and the score counts them as misses. That is the score working as
  written, and it is where the no-mistakes note hurt Haiku.
- **The grader is a Claude model too.** It never saw the arm or the instructions, but it is from
  the same family as the agents it scored.

### What happened

All 300 runs finished. None errored, and no other model answered.

#### The complex job: who handled it, of 10 runs per note

| Model | threat | bet | no mistakes | careful | plain note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haiku | 4 | 7 | 9 | 7 | 6 |
| Sonnet | 7 | 8 | 9 | 7 | **0** |
| **Both, of 20** | 11 | 15 | 18 | 14 | 6 |
| p against the plain note, both models | 0.20 | 0.010 | under 0.001 | 0.026 | |

The p row is the chance of a gap this big if the note made no difference; below 0.05 is the usual
bar. But four notes were each set against the same plain note, and four tries at one bar make a
fluke more likely. The plain fix for that is a stricter bar, 0.05 split four ways: 0.0125. The bet
and "no mistakes" clear it. The careful note (0.026) clears only the loose bar, and the threat (0.20)
clears neither: 11 against 6 is not a clear gain. All four lean on the same 6 of 20 plain-note
runs, so they are not four separate confirmations: one unlucky plain cell lifts them all together.

On Haiku alone no note was clearly apart from the plain one: "no mistakes" handled 9 against the
plain note's 6, the bet and the careful note 7, and the threat 4. Most of each gap comes from Sonnet: there, against
the plain note's 0 of 10, every note's gap has p 0.003 or lower. Those are four more
tests, picked after the run, on the same low plain-note cell, so the stricter bar doesn't cover them.

#### Sonnet's plain note did unusually badly

Sonnet's plain note missed all 10 runs. Every one emailed the 40-person list the config names
instead of the 4-person team that owns the export. Those runs also rarely read the schedule (1 of
10) or warned that the nightly rewrite could undo the fix (3 of 10).

That cell does not match the first run. There, the same plain note, on the same job, with the same
files and the same 14-turn limit, handled 7 of 15 runs on Sonnet (2, 2 and 3 of 5, in three
separate batches); the other 8 made this same mistake. Going from 7 of 15 to 0 of 10 would happen by chance about 2 times in 100. The runs were
shuffled, so the notes took turns and none ran as a block, and I found nothing else that changed.
So either this cell came out low by chance, or something about Sonnet shifted between the two runs.
I can't tell which from these runs. The gap on the hard job rests on that cell either way, and the
next step is to rerun Sonnet's plain note before leaning on it.

One more difference sits under the whole hard-job result. The simple and middle tasks name the team
to tell (finance-reporting); the hard task only says to let finance know. So the hard job is the
only one with a who-to-tell trap, and its gaps are about that trap, not about hard jobs in general.

#### What it says

As I expected before the runs, the simple and middle jobs showed a gap in how the agent worked,
not in whether it got the job done: every note took more turns on Sonnet, most did on Haiku, and some made
Haiku ask more questions.

The hard job is less clear than the first table makes it look. On Sonnet every note beat the plain
one, the careful note with no pressure in it included, but the plain note scored far below its own
first-run result. This run can't tell a reminder from pressure: the best note, "no mistakes", is a
pressure note, and the notes were never compared with each other.

The decision stays the same: I'm still not adding a threat, a bet or "no mistakes". Nothing here
shows a threat helps, and the clearer gains rest on one low plain-note cell. Naming the judgment
call (tell the team that owns it, not the whole list) still says what every note only hints at.

#### Limits of this run

- **Two models at medium effort,** one made-up world per kind of job, 10 runs per note in each.
  One model on one job needs a gap of about 5 runs to clear the bar.
- **The Sonnet result rests on one trap,** who to tell, and on one plain-note cell of 10 runs
  that came out far below the first run's. A job with a different hard part might not react the
  same way.
- **Four notes against one plain note,** with no test between the notes themselves.
- **A scripted world.** The agent picked actions from a menu and never ran anything real.
- **The grader is a Claude model too,** blind to the note and the model.

about a dollar.
