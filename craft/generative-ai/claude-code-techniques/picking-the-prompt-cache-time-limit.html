Tip: Ask Claude to Optimize Your Cache TTL

Questions this post answers

  • Should I use the 5-minute or the 1-hour prompt cache in Claude Code?
  • How do I set the prompt cache time limit in Claude Code?
  • How do I measure the pauses between my Claude Code turns?

What I observed​

Do you use Claude Code with an API key? If so, this page is for you.

At work we access Claude, but not through the monthly subscription. Instead, we get a bucket of API tokens. After burning through my bucket a couple of times, I compared it with my personal $200 a month Max plan. To match what that plan gives me, I'd need about $7,000 a month in credits. I figured tokens were just more expensive.

Then I came across a thread on r/ClaudeAI arguing that anyone who pauses 15 to 45 minutes between turns should switch the prompt cache from its 5-minute default to 1 hour. The reasoning was neat: the 1-hour cache costs more to fill, but one return after a coffee break pays that back. I pause like that all day. I step away while a long task runs, I sit in a meeting, and I keep 12 to 16 sessions running across three or four iTerm tabs, hopping between them.

But I had no idea what my pauses really looked like, or which limit Claude Code was even using for me. I was about to change a setting on the strength of a hunch.

Explained simply​

Every message you send Claude Code carries the whole conversation with it. Anthropic keeps a copy of that conversation, the prompt cache, so the next message can read it back cheaply instead of sending it all again.

  • Writing to the cache has two prices. A copy kept for 5 minutes costs 1.25 times the normal input price to write. A copy kept for 1 hour costs 2 times.
  • Reading it back costs the same either way, and it's cheap: a tenth of the input price or less.
  • A miss means writing it all again. Come back after the copy has expired and the whole conversation is written to the cache again, at the write price.
  • So to cut the cost, cut the misses. A pause of 5 to 60 minutes is a miss on the 5-minute cache and a cheap read on the 1-hour one. If you pause like that often, the 1-hour cache's higher write price pays for itself. If you rarely pause, or your pauses run past an hour, it doesn't.

You don't have to change how you work. If you regularly step away from Claude for 10 to 30 minutes at a time, your 5-minute cache has expired by the time you come back, but a 1-hour cache is still there.

For example, on Opus 5.5 a 100,000-token conversation costs $0.50 to write to the 5-minute cache, $0.80 to write to the 1-hour cache, and $0.02 to read back from either. Come back after 20 minutes: the 5-minute cache has expired and writes it all again for $0.50, while the 1-hour cache reads it back for $0.02.

What I did about it​

I read the docs to find out which limit applies when, then had Claude measure every pause in my own session logs and work out what each limit would have cost. The answer surprised me twice. On my subscription it is a wash. At work, on API credits, had I known about this setting, I could have saved about 10% all time, and about 13% on my most expensive sessions, the top quarter by cost. Then I built three calculators so you can try your own pattern before you change anything.

The technique: pick the prompt cache's time limit to fit how you work, and measure your pauses before you pick.

How the cache works is on its own page, How Claude Code's Prompt Cache Works, with the calculators:

  • What the cache holds: the block every request sends, and why it grows all session.
  • The two time limits: the prices, and why one return after a 5 to 60 minute pause pays for the 1-hour cache.
  • Try a day of pauses and what a whole session costs: the calculators.

What my own logs said​

Measured October 21 to 28, 2026. List prices, main conversation only.

I had Claude write a small script that reads my Claude Code session logs, sorts every request by time and puts the pause before each one into one of three groups: up to 5 minutes, 5 to 60 minutes, and over an hour. Then it works out what the same requests would have cost on the other limit. Only totals come out.

My breaks at work​

The savings come from my pauses, so I measured them in the same 47 meaningful sessions. A break runs from Claude's last line to my next message. Tool results and command output don't count as my messages. If I stopped Claude, the break starts when I stopped it.

How long they lasted

Most of my breaks are short: 63% under 5 minutes, where either cache is still warm. About a quarter fall between 5 and 60 minutes, the only stretch where 1 hour beats 5 minutes. The last 11% run past an hour, and by then both caches are gone.

It goes after that last 11%: coming back just before the hour instead of just after.

When they began

The breaks over 5 minutes follow a work day. They climb through the morning, peak at noon, and pick up again around 4 to 5pm. Most fall Tuesday to Thursday. They're the ordinary interruptions of a work day: I step away while Claude's answer waits, and on a 5-minute cache the whole conversation is written again when I come back.

The result​

The same setting came out even on my subscription and about a tenth cheaper at work, and 13% cheaper on my most expensive work sessions. The difference is the pauses. My heaviest work sessions each had 10 or more returns after a 5 to 60 minute pause, and on a 5-minute cache every one of those returns writes the whole conversation again. A 1-hour cache isn't the whole gap between my work credits and my subscription. But on an API key it's one setting, and my long sessions say it pays.

Words of caution​

Don't change these settings blindly. The 1-hour cache only pays when your main conversation sits idle for 5 to 60 minutes and then picks up again. For many people it doesn't.

  • You answer Claude right away. If you wait on each reply and answer every question as it comes, your main conversation never sits for 5 minutes. The 5-minute cache is cheaper for you.
  • You auto-approve everything. If you skip the permission checks and let Claude run without stopping for you, it rarely waits on you at all. The 5-minute cache is likely cheaper for you too.
  • The 1-hour cache is for waiting. Consider it only if your main conversation often waits on you for more than 5 minutes, or waits on subagents that take more than 5 minutes to finish.

Measure first, with the prompt below, and change the setting only if your own logs say so.

Changing your habits instead​

The setting fits the cache to how you work. You can also fit how you work to the cache. From cheapest to least cheap:

  1. Never leave a session idle for 5 minutes, and stay on the 5-minute cache. The cheapest write price, and almost no misses.
  2. Never leave a session idle for an hour, and use the 1-hour cache. Coffee breaks and short meetings stay cheap reads, but every write costs more.
  3. Keep subagents under an hour when the main conversation waits on them. A subagent that runs longer lets even the 1-hour cache expire, so the main conversation writes everything again when it comes back.

Set it up​

Check which limit you're on. In Claude Code 2.1.251 or later, /usage shows a "Prompt cache (main)" line with your hit ratio, how many times the cache was missed, and whether it is still warm. For which limit you're on, send one message and look at the usage:

claude -p "hello" --output-format json | jq '.usage.cache_creation'

Whichever of ephemeral_1h_input_tokens and ephemeral_5m_input_tokens is above zero is the limit you're on.

Which limit applies by default (Claude Code docs):

  • Main conversation on a Claude subscription, within your plan: 1 hour.
  • Main conversation on an API key, a cloud provider, or usage credits: 5 minutes.
  • Subagents, compaction and other helpers: 5 minutes. On a subscription, a few helper requests that Anthropic's servers control get 1 hour.

Change it (Claude Code 2.1.242 or later) in one of two ways:

  • In settings.json
  • With environment variables

For every session, add these to ~/.claude/settings.json:

{
  "promptCacheTtl": "1h",
  "subagentPromptCacheTtl": "5m"
}

For one shell, export these before you start claude:

export CLAUDE_CODE_PROMPT_CACHE_TTL=1h
export CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_PROMPT_CACHE_TTL=5m

Put them in your shell profile to keep them.

FORCE_PROMPT_CACHING_5M=1 overrides both. The full order is on the prompt caching page and the keys are in the settings reference.

Calling the API yourself? Ask for 1 hour on the block you cache: "cache_control": {"type": "ephemeral", "ttl": "1h"}. See prompt caching in the API docs.

Decide whether to switch. Claude Code keeps each session as a .jsonl file under ~/.claude/projects/, so your own pauses are already on disk. Here are three ways to read them; pick one.

  • Ask Claude
  • Run the script
  • Count pauses with jq

Paste this into a Claude Code session:

I want you to analyze how I take breaks and tell me whether my prompt cache time limits are set
right for them.
First read Claude Code's docs on prompt caching and on settings:
https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching and https://code.claude.com/docs/en/settings.
Then read this script, which prices my session logs at both limits:
https://gist.github.com/omars-lab/4fc25fd8209d7b2199821ad84988a253
Use it, or the same method, on my session logs (the .jsonl files under ~/.claude/projects/).
Look at my breaks: how often I step away and for how long, and whether my usage pattern leans
toward the 5-minute or the 1-hour cache.
In the process, tell me which limit saves me more money and tokens, for the main conversation
and for subagents: the share of my spend I would save, and how many tokens I would stop writing
to the cache a second time. Then tell me whether I should change promptCacheTtl or
subagentPromptCacheTtl.

Reading the docs first means Claude works from the current defaults and setting names, not from what it remembers. The script gives it a tested way to work out the saving instead of guessing.

The script I used for the numbers above is public as a gist, and its full code is in the appendix. It needs Node 20 and nothing else, reads the logs on the machine you run it on, and prints totals only. If your logs ran at 5 minutes (an API key, or subagents), it answers "with 1 hour, you would have saved N% of your budget". If they ran at 1 hour, it prices them at 5 minutes. Download it with the GitHub CLI and run it:

gh gist view 4fc25fd8209d7b2199821ad84988a253 --raw --filename cache-ttl-gaps.mjs > cache-ttl-gaps.mjs
node cache-ttl-gaps.mjs --root ~/.claude --days 30
# logs too old to say 5m or 1h: add --ran-at 5m

This counts the pause before every request, across all your sessions (it needs jq):

for f in ~/.claude/projects/*/*.jsonl; do
  jq -rs '[.[] | select(.type == "assistant" and .message.usage)]
    | unique_by(.message.id)
    | map(.timestamp[:19] + "Z" | fromdateiso8601) | sort | .[]' "$f" 2>/dev/null |
  awk 'NR > 1 { g = ($1 - p) / 60; if (g <= 5) a++; else if (g <= 60) b++; else c++ }
       { p = $1 } END { print a + 0, b + 0, c + 0 }'
done | awk '{ a += $1; b += $2; c += $3 }
  END { printf "up to 5 min: %d\n5 to 60 min: %d\nover 60 min: %d\n", a, b, c }'

The log format isn't documented, so check it still works after an update. If the middle line is a real share of the total and your prompts are big, try 1 hour for a few days and compare /usage.

What it looks like​

That's the prompt from Set it up, typed and ready to send. I got there the long way: I asked for this page, pasting in the Reddit reasoning, and before writing it Claude went through my own logs. The numbers changed what the page says: I came in expecting "switch to 1 hour" and left with "measure first": it paid at work and made no difference at home.

Why it works​

  • The cost follows your pauses, not the setting. The same 1-hour limit made no difference on my subscription and saved about a tenth at work. Only your own pause pattern says which.
  • A pause is cheap to measure. Every request is already in your session logs with a time on it.
  • The break-even is simple. 1 hour costs 0.75x more on what it writes and saves about 1.15x on each return after 5 to 60 minutes (the two time limits). If those returns are common next to how fast your conversation grows, 1 hour wins.
  • Subagents are a separate choice. They work in fast bursts (99% of my subagent requests came within 5 minutes of the last), which is why they default to 5 minutes.
  • Waiting on a subagent is a pause too. The main conversation's cache clock runs while a subagent works. Subagents that take 5 to 60 minutes favour 1 hour on the main conversation; ones that finish in under 5 minutes, or take over an hour, don't.
